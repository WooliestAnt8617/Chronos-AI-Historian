MEDIEVAL HISTORY

THE BYZANTINE EMPIRE

The Byzantine Empire, the eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, lasted from the fall of Rome in 476 CE until 1453 CE — nearly a thousand years. Its capital, Constantinople (modern-day Istanbul), was founded by Emperor Constantine I in 330 CE on the site of the ancient Greek city of Byzantium.

Emperor Justinian I (reigned 527-565 CE) oversaw the empire's greatest territorial expansion, reconquering North Africa, Italy, and parts of Spain. He commissioned the construction of the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, completed in 537 CE. This architectural masterpiece featured a massive dome spanning 31 meters in diameter and remained the world's largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years.

Justinian also ordered the codification of Roman law into the Corpus Juris Civilis (Body of Civil Law), which became the foundation of legal systems across Europe and influenced modern civil law in many countries worldwide.

The Byzantine Empire preserved Greek and Roman knowledge through the Dark Ages, maintaining libraries, schools, and scholarly traditions when much of Western Europe had lost access to classical learning. Byzantine scholars translated and preserved works by Aristotle, Plato, Euclid, and other ancient thinkers.

Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks under Sultan Mehmed II on May 29, 1453, after a 53-day siege. The fall of Constantinople is often cited as the event marking the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the Modern Era.

THE CRUSADES

The Crusades were a series of religious wars sanctioned by the Latin Church between the 11th and 13th centuries. Pope Urban II launched the First Crusade in 1095 CE at the Council of Clermont, calling upon Christian knights to recapture the Holy Land from Muslim rule.

The First Crusade (1096-1099) resulted in the capture of Jerusalem on July 15, 1099. The Crusaders established four Crusader states: the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Edessa. An estimated 60,000-100,000 Crusaders participated in the First Crusade.

Saladin (Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, 1137-1193), the Sultan of Egypt and Syria, recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders on October 2, 1187, after the Battle of Hattin. His chivalry and military prowess earned him respect from both Muslim and Christian chroniclers.

The Third Crusade (1189-1192), led by Richard I of England ("Richard the Lionheart"), Philip II of France, and Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa, failed to recapture Jerusalem but secured a treaty allowing Christian pilgrims access to the holy city.

The Fourth Crusade (1202-1204) infamously diverted from its original target and sacked Constantinople, the Christian capital of the Byzantine Empire, in April 1204. This event deepened the rift between the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches.

There were nine major Crusades in total, spanning from 1096 to 1291, when the fall of Acre marked the end of Crusader presence in the Holy Land.

FEUDALISM IN MEDIEVAL EUROPE

Feudalism was the dominant social and economic system in medieval Europe from roughly the 9th to the 15th century. The system was based on the exchange of land (fiefs) for military service and loyalty. At the top stood the king, who granted land to nobles (lords), who in turn granted portions to lesser nobles (vassals), who oversaw peasants and serfs.

The feudal system emerged in response to the instability following the fall of the Roman Empire and the Viking, Magyar, and Saracen invasions of the 9th and 10th centuries. Lords built castles for defense — by the 11th century, there were an estimated 10,000 castles across Europe.

Serfs made up approximately 85-90% of the medieval European population. They were bound to the land they worked and could not leave without their lord's permission. In exchange for protection and the right to farm, serfs owed their lords labor (typically three days per week), a portion of their harvest, and various fees.

The feudal system began to decline after the Black Death (1347-1351), which killed an estimated 75-200 million people in Eurasia — roughly 30-60% of Europe's population. The massive labor shortage that followed gave surviving peasants greater bargaining power, leading to higher wages and the gradual erosion of serfdom.

THE MONGOL EMPIRE

The Mongol Empire, founded by Genghis Khan (born Temüjin) in 1206, became the largest contiguous land empire in history, eventually covering approximately 24 million square kilometers — about 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Genghis Khan (c. 1162-1227) united the Mongol tribes and launched devastating campaigns across Central Asia, China, Persia, and Eastern Europe. His army, though typically numbering only 100,000-130,000 warriors, defeated forces many times its size through superior military tactics, including feigned retreats, psychological warfare, and the use of siege engineers captured from conquered peoples.

The Mongol Empire established the Yam system, a vast postal and communication network spanning the empire. Relay stations were placed every 25-30 miles, allowing messages to travel up to 200 miles per day. This system facilitated trade along the Silk Road, leading to an unprecedented exchange of goods, ideas, and technologies between East and West.

After Genghis Khan's death in 1227, the empire was divided among his sons and grandsons into four khanates: the Golden Horde, the Chagatai Khanate, the Ilkhanate, and the Yuan Dynasty in China. His grandson Kublai Khan (1215-1294) completed the conquest of China and established the Yuan Dynasty, which ruled China from 1271 to 1368.
