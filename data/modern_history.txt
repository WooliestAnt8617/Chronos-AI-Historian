MODERN HISTORY

THE RENAISSANCE (14TH-17TH CENTURY)

The Renaissance, meaning "rebirth" in French, was a cultural, intellectual, and artistic movement that began in Italy in the 14th century and spread throughout Europe over the next three centuries. It marked the transition from the medieval period to the early modern era.

Florence, Italy, was the birthplace of the Renaissance, largely due to the patronage of the wealthy Medici family. Cosimo de' Medici (1389-1464) and his grandson Lorenzo the Magnificent (1449-1492) funded artists, architects, and scholars, transforming Florence into the cultural capital of Europe.

Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519) epitomized the Renaissance ideal of the "universal man." He painted the Mona Lisa (c. 1503-1519) and The Last Supper (1495-1498), while also producing detailed anatomical drawings, engineering designs for flying machines, and studies of hydraulics. His notebooks contained over 7,000 pages of observations and inventions.

Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475-1564) sculpted the David (completed in 1504) and painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel (1508-1512), which covers approximately 500 square meters and depicts over 300 figures from biblical narratives.

Johannes Gutenberg's invention of the movable-type printing press around 1440 in Mainz, Germany, revolutionized the spread of knowledge. By 1500, an estimated 20 million volumes had been printed in Europe, compared to the relatively few manuscripts that existed before. This technological advance made books affordable and accessible, fueling the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution.

THE SCIENTIFIC REVOLUTION

The Scientific Revolution (approximately 1543-1687) fundamentally changed how humans understood the natural world, replacing ancient Greek and medieval thinking with empirical observation and mathematical reasoning.

Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543) published "De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium" in 1543, proposing the heliocentric model of the solar system — that the Earth and other planets revolve around the Sun. This challenged the geocentric Ptolemaic model that had dominated astronomy for over 1,400 years.

Galileo Galilei (1564-1642), often called the "father of modern science," used an improved telescope to observe the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, and sunspots. His support of heliocentrism led to his trial by the Roman Inquisition in 1633, after which he was placed under house arrest for the remainder of his life.

Isaac Newton (1643-1727) published "Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica" in 1687, establishing the laws of motion and universal gravitation. This work unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics and remained the foundation of physics until Einstein's theory of relativity in the 20th century. Newton also co-developed calculus independently of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.

THE ENLIGHTENMENT

The Enlightenment (approximately 1685-1815), also known as the Age of Reason, was an intellectual movement that emphasized reason, individualism, and skepticism of traditional authority. It originated in Europe and profoundly influenced political revolutions and the development of modern democratic societies.

Key Enlightenment thinkers included John Locke (1632-1704), who argued for natural rights (life, liberty, and property) and the social contract theory of government. His ideas directly influenced Thomas Jefferson's drafting of the American Declaration of Independence in 1776.

Voltaire (1694-1778) championed freedom of speech, separation of church and state, and religious tolerance. His satirical works criticized the French establishment and the Catholic Church. Baron de Montesquieu (1689-1755) proposed the separation of governmental powers into legislative, executive, and judicial branches in his 1748 work "The Spirit of the Laws," which influenced the structure of the United States government.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778) argued that civilization had corrupted humanity's natural goodness and advocated for popular sovereignty. His work "The Social Contract" (1762) opened with the famous line, "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."

THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION

The Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain around 1760 and continued until approximately 1840, fundamentally transforming manufacturing, transportation, and society. It marked the shift from hand production methods to machine manufacturing.

James Watt's improvements to the steam engine in the 1760s and 1770s were pivotal. His separate condenser design (patented in 1769) made the steam engine practical for industrial use. By 1800, there were approximately 2,500 steam engines operating in Britain.

The textile industry was the first to be industrialized. Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785) mechanized spinning and weaving. Cotton production in Britain increased from 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to 366 million pounds by 1830.

The revolution brought massive urbanization. Manchester's population grew from 25,000 in 1772 to over 300,000 by 1850. London's population exceeded 2.3 million by 1850, making it the world's largest city. However, rapid urbanization brought overcrowding, pollution, and poor working conditions, especially for children — some as young as five worked 14-16 hour days in factories.

George Stephenson's Rocket locomotive (1829) demonstrated the viability of steam-powered rail transport. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, was the first inter-city railway line. By 1850, Britain had over 6,000 miles of railway track.

COLONIALISM AND IMPERIALISM

European colonialism expanded dramatically from the 15th to the 20th century. The Age of Exploration, beginning with Portuguese and Spanish voyages in the 15th century, led to European control over vast territories in the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.

Christopher Columbus's voyage in 1492, funded by Queen Isabella I and King Ferdinand II of Spain, led to European awareness of the Americas. The subsequent colonization devastated indigenous populations — it is estimated that the indigenous population of the Americas declined by approximately 90% between 1492 and 1600, primarily due to diseases like smallpox, measles, and influenza to which they had no immunity.

The British Empire, at its height in the early 20th century, covered approximately 35.5 million square kilometers — about 25% of the world's land surface — and governed over 400 million people. The phrase "the empire on which the sun never sets" reflected its global reach.

The Scramble for Africa (1881-1914) saw European powers carve up nearly the entire African continent. The Berlin Conference of 1884-1885, attended by 14 European nations, established rules for the partition of Africa. By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent of European control.

The French Revolution (1789-1799) began on July 14, 1789, with the storming of the Bastille prison in Paris. It overthrew the monarchy, established a republic, and culminated in Napoleon Bonaparte's rise to power. The revolution's ideals of "liberté, égalité, fraternité" (liberty, equality, fraternity) spread across Europe and influenced independence movements worldwide.
