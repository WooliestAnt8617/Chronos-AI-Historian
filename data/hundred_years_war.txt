THE HUNDRED YEARS' WAR (1337–1453)

The Hundred Years' War was a series of intermittent conflicts between the House of Plantagenet (rulers of the Kingdom of England) and the House of Valois (rulers of the Kingdom of France) over the right to rule the Kingdom of France. It lasted 116 years and was one of the most significant conflicts of the Middle Ages.

CAUSES OF THE WAR
The war was not a single continuous conflict but a series of linked wars triggered by several factors:
1. Dynastic Dispute: The main trigger was the death of the French King Charles IV in 1328 without a male heir. Edward III of England, whose mother Isabella was Charles's sister, claimed the French throne. However, French nobles chose Philip VI of Valois, invoking Salic Law which supposedly barred females and their descendants from the throne.
2. The Duchy of Aquitaine: The English kings held the Duchy of Aquitaine as vassals to the French crown. Constant friction over French legal interference in the duchy led Philip VI to declare the duchy forfeit in 1337, which Edward III met by declaring himself the rightful King of France.
3. Economic Interests: Flanders was a major market for English wool but was technically a French fief. Conflict over control of this wealthy region added to the tensions.

KEY PHASES AND EVENTS
- The Edwardian War (1337–1360): Notable for English victories at the Battle of Crécy (1346) and the Battle of Poitiers (1356), where the French King John II was captured. The Treaty of Brétigny (1360) granted Edward III significant territories in France in exchange for renouncing his claim to the throne.
- The Caroline War (1369–1389): Under Charles V, the French regained most of the territory lost in the previous phase.
- The Lancastrian War (1415–1453): Henry V of England invaded France and won a massive victory at the Battle of Agincourt (1415). By the Treaty of Troyes (1420), he was recognized as the heir to the French throne. However, the emergence of Joan of Arc in 1429 turned the tide. She inspired the French to lift the Siege of Orléans and crown Charles VII at Reims.

JOAN OF ARC
Joan of Arc, a peasant girl who claimed divine guidance, became a pivotal figure. Her leadership revitalized French morale. Although she was captured by the Burgundians, sold to the English, and burned at the stake for heresy in 1431, her impact was lasting. She was later canonized as a saint.

ENDING AND IMPACT
The war ended in 1453 when the French captured Bordeaux, leaving the English with only the port of Calais on mainland France.
- Military Evolution: The war saw the decline of heavy cavalry and the rise of the longbow, and later, the introduction of gunpowder and cannons.
- National Identity: The conflict helped forge distinct national identities for both England and France.
- Social Change: The heavy taxes and devastation led to social unrest, such as the Peasants' Revolt in England (1381).
